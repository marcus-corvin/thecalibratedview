Notes

These notes point the curious reader toward the research and thinking behind each chapter. They are not exhaustive; they are doorways. Books appear in italics with the year of first publication. Journal articles appear in quotation marks with the journal named, so that you can tell which claims rest on a book-length argument and which rest on a single paper. Where the text makes a claim that no single source carries, the note says so rather than borrowing authority from a nearby name.

A word on what this book is not. It is not therapy, and if you carry wounds that need clinical attention, nothing here replaces that care. It takes no political position and asks no tribal allegiance. It is scaffolding, built on the sources below and tested against ordinary life. Use it, and set it down.

Spring: The 9 Lenses

The Family Lens. John Bowlby, Attachment and Loss, volume one, Attachment (1969), argued that relational patterns form before a child can speak. Mary Ainsworth’s Strange Situation procedure and the secure, avoidant and anxious classifications are set out in Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters and Wall, Patterns of Attachment (1978). Murray Bowen, Family Therapy in Clinical Practice (1978), extends the argument from the pair to the household. The chapter now leads with Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, The Social Construction of Reality (1966), whose account of primary socialization covers the same ground without depending on a contested experimental literature: the child does not learn one possible world, it learns the world, and what is learned first is not experienced as learning at all. On the transmission of attachment from parent to child, the honest figure is smaller than the body of research first suggested; the literature calls the shortfall the transmission gap, named in Marinus van IJzendoorn’s meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin (1995). The family is a living structure rather than a collection of individuals, and every member is shaped by its architecture.

The Cultural Lens. The 96% figure is Jeffrey Arnett’s, from “The Neglected 95%: Why American Psychology Needs to Become Less American”, a paper in American Psychologist (2008). Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan, “The Weirdest People in the World?”, a paper in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (2010), supplied the WEIRD acronym and documented how far those populations sit from the human range; Henrich’s book of the similar title, The WEIRDest People in the World, came in 2020 and is a separate work. Pierre Bourdieu supplies the frame this chapter now rests on. Outline of a Theory of Practice (1977) sets out habitus, the durable dispositions laid down by social position that generate behaviour without being consulted, and doxa, the part of a social world so taken for granted that it is not experienced as a belief at all. Hysteresis, the lag when a habitus formed in one setting meets another, appears there and in The Logic of Practice (1990). Geert Hofstede, Culture’s Consequences (1980), mapped the invisible operating systems that cultures install in their members, not preferences but perceptual defaults. The cross-cultural emotion research referenced in the chapter is a body of work rather than a single study, and no one paper is being leaned on here.

The Trauma Lens. Bessel van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014), reframed trauma as a perceptual filter rather than only a wound. The Adverse Childhood Experiences study, with roughly 17,000 participants, was first reported in Vincent Felitti, Robert Anda and colleagues, “Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults”, a paper in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine (1998). Peter Levine, Waking the Tiger (1997), describes how the body holds protective responses long after the threat has passed. These are not memories in the usual sense. They are lenses.

The Information Lens. Cultivation theory comes from George Gerbner and Larry Gross, “Living with Television: The Violence Profile”, a paper in the Journal of Communication (1976), which found that heavy viewers perceive the world as more dangerous than it is; the measured effects are small, so read it as a real tilt rather than a large one. Neil Postman, Amusing Ourselves to Death (1985), argues that the medium shapes the message, which shapes the mind. Eli Pariser, The Filter Bubble (2011), names what algorithmic curation does to perception. The availability heuristic is Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, “Availability: A Heuristic for Judging Frequency and Probability”, a paper in Cognitive Psychology (1973), and is treated at book length in Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). What is memorable feels frequent, whether or not it is.

The Body Lens. Lisa Feldman Barrett, How Emotions Are Made (2017), sets out constructed emotion theory. Emotions are assembled from bodily signals, past experience and context rather than triggered as hardwired reactions. The wider embodied-cognition literature is the shakiest ground in this book: several of its best-known demonstrations, including the warm-cup and pre-lunch judging studies, have either failed to replicate or been shown to rest on a confound, and this chapter has been rewritten to rest on Barrett’s account instead. Lawrence Barsalou’s grounded-cognition papers, in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (1999) and the Annual Review of Psychology (2008), are the framework rather than any single demonstration, and the framework is in far better shape than the demonstrations were. Russell Foster’s circadian work supports the narrower claim the chapter now makes: attention, reaction time and error rates move on a daily curve. The chapter no longer claims that moral judgement does. This is the part of the argument I am least confident about.

The Pivot. Marcus Aurelius wrote the Meditations (composed c. 170 AD) as private notebooks, self-examination by a man with absolute power who chose to question his own seeing. The chapter reads him against the record rather than at his own valuation. The persecution at Lyon in 177, in which Christians were tortured and killed under his authority, is recorded by Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History, book five; the Meditations do not mention it. Etty Hillesum’s diaries and letters, written in occupied Amsterdam between 1941 and 1943 and published in English as An Interrupted Life, document something rarer, the observing self maintained under extreme external pressure.

The Performed Self vs. The Observing Self. Arthur Deikman, The Observing Self (1982), gives the concept its name. William James distinguishes the “me” from the “I” in The Principles of Psychology (1890). The self-as-context model comes from Steven Hayes, Kirk Strosahl and Kelly Wilson, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (1999). The mirror self-recognition paradigm begins with Gordon Gallup Jr., “Chimpanzees: Self-Recognition”, a paper in Science (1970); the developmental work tracing when human infants first pass it is a literature rather than one study, and the chapter uses it as illustration, not as evidence.

The Observer Is Also Installed. The objection this section answers was aimed at Pierre Bourdieu long before it was aimed at this book, and his answer is reflexive sociology: the analyst’s own position is itself an object of analysis, set out in Bourdieu and Loïc Wacquant, An Invitation to Reflexive Sociology (1992). The replication figures quoted are from the Open Science Collaboration’s “Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science”, a paper in Science (2015), which repeated one hundred studies and reproduced fewer than half. The argument that a theoretical claim is not vulnerable in the same way is mine, and it is a claim about what kind of thing a theory is rather than a finding.

The Lens Somebody Else Can See. No single study grounds this section. The structural argument, that a filter you can detect is already half-removed and therefore cannot be the one doing the most work, is the chapter’s own. Where it touches research it touches the same sources as the rest of the season, and where it goes beyond them it says so.

The Practice of Noticing. The claim here is the ordinary one from the psychotherapy literature, that insight by itself is a poor predictor of changed behaviour. Peter Gollwitzer, “Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans”, a paper in American Psychologist (1999), is the useful counterpart. An insight converted into a specific plan, when X happens I will do Y, survives contact with an ordinary week far better than an insight left as a resolution. Jon Kabat-Zinn, Full Catastrophe Living (1990), reports thirty years of work showing that repeated practice matters more than single breakthroughs. Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) names the two speeds of thinking this chapter asks you to notice. Affect labelling is Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, “Putting Feelings Into Words”, a paper in Psychological Science (2007). Naming an emotion dampens the amygdala response. Seeing changes the thing you see.

Feeling vs. Seeing. Lieberman’s affect-labelling work, cited above, supplies the neural evidence. James Gross, editor, Handbook of Emotion Regulation (2007), collects the reappraisal research showing that you do not have to stop feeling to start seeing. Antonio Damasio, Descartes’ Error (1994), reframes emotions as decision-support data rather than decision-makers.

The Self You Don’t Show. Carl Jung’s concept of the shadow runs across his Collected Works rather than sitting in one volume, and is best approached through Aion (1951). Brené Brown, Daring Greatly (2012), describes the energy cost of suppressing hidden parts of the self; the experimental support for that cost is Daniel Wegner and colleagues’ thought-suppression work, “Paradoxical Effects of Thought Suppression”, a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (1987), the white-bear studies. The expressive-writing finding is James Pennebaker and Sandra Beall, “Confronting a Traumatic Event”, a paper in the Journal of Abnormal Psychology (1986), developed in Pennebaker, Opening Up (1990). Fifteen to twenty minutes of private writing produced measurable effects, though later work has found them smaller and more variable than the early reports suggested.

Summer: The 9 Encounters

Opening. The meeting of Rumi and Shams of Tabriz at Konya in 1244 is reconstructed in Franklin Lewis, Rumi: Past and Present, East and West (2000), which is also the best guide to how much of the story is documented and how much is later tradition. It changed the direction of Rumi’s life and work, not through teaching, but through encounter.

The Projection Lens. Projection as a defence mechanism is Freud’s, systematised by Anna Freud in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). Confirmation bias begins with Peter Wason, “On the Failure to Eliminate Hypotheses in a Conceptual Task”, a paper in the Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology (1960), and is surveyed in Raymond Nickerson, “Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises”, a paper in the Review of General Psychology (1998). Once you have a story about someone, you notice what confirms it and filter out what contradicts it. Projection and confirmation bias together make a nearly airtight loop.

The Expectation Trap. Judee Burgoon’s expectancy violations theory, developed across communication research from the late 1970s, explains why we react more strongly when people deviate from our expectations than when they confirm them; it is a body of work rather than one paper. The fundamental attribution error, overweighting character and underweighting situation, was named by Lee Ross in “The Intuitive Psychologist and His Shortcomings”, a chapter in Advances in Experimental Social Psychology (1977).

Love Without Projection. John Gottman and Nan Silver, The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work (1999), sets out four decades of couples research on bids for connection, turning toward, and the ratio of positive to negative interactions. Esther Perel, Mating in Captivity (2006), explores why desire and security want different things.

The Stranger Test. Stanley Milgram first reported the obedience experiments in “Behavioral Study of Obedience”, a paper in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology (1963), and at book length in Obedience to Authority (1974). The bystander research is John Darley and Bibb Latané, “Bystander Intervention in Emergencies: Diffusion of Responsibility”, a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (1968), and their book The Unresponsive Bystander (1970). Integrity is not what you believe about yourself. It is what you do when no one is keeping score and the situation is arranged to make the right thing inconvenient.

The Other Side of Listening. Carl Rogers, On Becoming a Person (1961), defines accurate empathic understanding as the most healing act in human interaction. William Ickes, editor, Empathic Accuracy (1997), collects the research on the gap between how well people think they listen and how well they do. The resonance system the chapter describes draws on mirror-neuron work surveyed in Giacomo Rizzolatti and Laila Craighero, “The Mirror-Neuron System”, a paper in the Annual Review of Neuroscience (2004); whether humans have the system in the form described is still argued over. The mechanism here is less settled than the finding, and this note states the finding.

Attention as Architecture. William James wrote in The Principles of Psychology (1890) that attention is “the taking possession by the mind”. The learning principle behind “what fires together wires together” is Donald Hebb, The Organization of Behavior (1949). Tristan Harris’s work at the Center for Humane Technology documents how variable reward schedules, social validation loops and infinite scroll turn that biological fact into a business model; it is public advocacy and reporting rather than a peer-reviewed literature, and is cited here as such.

The Work You Do vs. The Work That Does You. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Flow (1990), describes work at its best, skill meeting challenge and self-consciousness dissolving. Byung-Chul Han, The Burnout Society (2010 in German, 2015 in English), describes work at its worst, self-exploitation replacing external exploitation. Montaigne’s Essays (from 1580) offer the earliest modern exploration of busyness, retirement and the performing self.

Solitude vs. Isolation. Donald Winnicott, “The Capacity to be Alone”, a paper in the International Journal of Psycho-Analysis (1958), treats solitude as a marker of emotional maturity. John Cacioppo and William Patrick, Loneliness (2008), separates quantity of contact from quality of felt connection. It is possible to be surrounded and still isolated. Montaigne’s tower library remains the most famous example of chosen solitude as generative practice.

The World You Didn’t Build. Irvin Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy (1980), names the four givens every human confronts: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. The phenomenological demand to see things as they present themselves before theory edits them runs from Edmund Husserl through Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception (1945). Montaigne’s travel journal of 1580 and 1581 was an early, deliberate experiment in encountering the world beyond one’s assumptions.

Fall: The 9 Patterns

Opening. Stanley Milgram’s obedience experiments at Yale, begun in 1961, showed that ordinary people inside invisible patterns of authority would administer what they believed to be life-threatening shocks. The sixty-five percent figure belongs to one condition of roughly two dozen; across the variations obedience ranged from near zero to over ninety percent, which is the more useful finding, since it shows how much the pattern depends on its arrangement. Gina Perry, Behind the Shock Machine (2013), documents departures from the stated protocol and pressure applied to participants from the Yale archives, and is worth reading alongside Milgram’s own Obedience to Authority (1974). The experiment was about the pattern, not the person.

The Herd Lens. Solomon Asch’s conformity experiments are reported in “Effects of Group Pressure Upon the Modification and Distortion of Judgments”, a chapter in Groups, Leadership and Men (1951), and more accessibly in “Opinions and Social Pressure”, a paper in Scientific American (1955). Seventy-five percent of participants agreed with an obviously wrong group answer at least once; the per-trial rate was far lower, and the two numbers are often confused. Irving Janis, Victims of Groupthink (1972), named the phenomenon of unanimity overriding realistic appraisal. Social identity theory is Henri Tajfel and John Turner, “An Integrative Theory of Intergroup Conflict” (1979), which showed how quickly people become the group and adopt its perspective as their own.

The Belonging Price. Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann first set out the spiral of silence in “The Spiral of Silence: A Theory of Public Opinion”, a paper in the Journal of Communication (1974), and at book length in The Spiral of Silence (1984). Perceived minority opinion holders self-censor, and the majority view then appears more dominant than it is. Chris Argyris, Overcoming Organizational Defenses (1990), named the undiscussables, the topics a group knows about but refuses to address. Pluralistic ignorance is documented in Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller, “Pluralistic Ignorance and Alcohol Use on Campus”, a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (1993). Private disagreement is masked by assumed consensus, everyone silent because they believe everyone else agrees.

What Your Community Rewards. Émile Durkheim, The Division of Labour in Society (1893), is the foundation for social cohesion through shared norms enforced by reward and punishment. Community incentive structures do not reflect a community’s stated values. They reflect its deepest anxieties.

The Silence That Shapes. Eviatar Zerubavel, The Elephant in the Room (2006), examines socially organised denial, how communities agree not to see what is plainly visible. The collective memory tradition begins with Maurice Halbwachs, Les cadres sociaux de la mémoire (1925), available in English as On Collective Memory. Communities remember and forget together, and what they choose to forget shapes them as powerfully as what they remember.

The Patterns You Feed. Darley and Latané’s bystander work, cited above, and Hannah Arendt, Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963), with its account of the banality of evil, converge on one insight. The spectrum from active perpetration to passive non-examination is shorter than anyone wants to believe.

The Pivot / The Dignity Question. Desmond Tutu, No Future Without Forgiveness (1999), is his own account of chairing South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, whose hearings began in 1996 and which operationalised ubuntu, “I am because we are”, at national scale. Donna Hicks, Dignity (2011), brings twenty years of international conflict work to ten essential elements and the argument that violated dignity is the wound beneath every conflict. Emmanuel Levinas, Totality and Infinity (1961), places the encounter with the human face at the centre of ethics.

The Debt of Seeing. Lawrence Kohlberg, Essays on Moral Development (1981), maps the stages. Carol Gilligan, In a Different Voice (1982), reframed morality around relational responsibility. Darley and Latané’s three conditions for intervention, noticing, interpreting the event as an emergency, and feeling personally responsible, describe the mechanism by which seeing becomes owing.

How Communities Heal. Vamik Volkan, Bloodlines (1997), shows how communities pass unresolved grief across generations as chosen trauma, a wound that becomes identity. Howard Zehr, Changing Lenses (1990), is the founding statement of restorative justice and its argument that naming-centred processes outperform punitive ones in many contexts.

The Collective Mirror. Bowen’s family systems theory, cited under the Family Lens, applied at community scale, reveals that triangulation, emotional cutoff and differentiation repeat whether you are looking at a civilisation or a dinner table.

Winter: The 9 Forces

Opening. The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, demonstrating that systems which appear permanent are configurations sustained by collective belief, and that they can dissolve in hours when the configuration shifts.

The System Lens. Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems: A Primer (2008), is the clearest introduction. A system is a set of interconnected elements organised to achieve a function, which may differ from its stated purpose. Peter Senge, The Fifth Discipline (1990), maps the archetypes by which structures drift from intended to emergent purpose. You are always inside a system, and the system has its own logic.

The Money Filter. Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir, Scarcity (2013), documented the bandwidth tax. Poverty does not only limit resources; it consumes cognitive capacity, making every other form of seeing harder. Cross-country social mobility data is summarised in Miles Corak, “Income Inequality, Equality of Opportunity, and Intergenerational Mobility”, a paper in the Journal of Economic Perspectives (2013). Parental economic position remains among the strongest predictors of a child’s eventual position, and the strength varies sharply between countries.

The Power Map. Steven Lukes, Power: A Radical View (1974), distinguishes visible power, who wins; hidden power, who sets the agenda; and invisible power, who shapes what is thinkable. Michel Foucault, Discipline and Punish (1975), extends this into the architecture of daily life, where institutions produce subjects and discipline creates normalised behaviour without explicit coercion.

The Institution Trap. Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell, “The Iron Cage Revisited: Institutional Isomorphism and Collective Rationality in Organizational Fields”, a paper in the American Sociological Review (1983), explains why institutions come to resemble each other regardless of their stated missions. Robert Michels, Political Parties (1911), states the iron law of oligarchy. Every organisation, however democratic its origin, tends toward control by a small group. The institution trap is structural, not moral.

The Manufactured View. Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, Manufacturing Consent (1988), identified five filters through which mass media shapes perception. Nick Srnicek, Platform Capitalism (2016), and Shoshana Zuboff, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (2019), extend the analysis to algorithmic curation optimised for engagement rather than truth. Miranda Fricker, Epistemic Injustice (2007), names the systematic discounting of certain people’s knowledge. Some voices never reach the view.

The Long View. Stewart Brand’s pace layers framework (fashion, commerce, infrastructure, governance, culture, nature) appears in The Clock of the Long Now (1999). Different layers of civilisation move at different speeds, so what feels like crisis at the fashion layer may be invisible at the culture layer, and the reverse. Telling cyclical change from structural change is among the most consequential perceptual skills available, and no single source is claimed for that judgement.

The Climate of Your Life. Demographic transition theory, the history of technological disruption from the printing press to the internet, and ecological change at civilisational timescales all inform this chapter; each is a field rather than a citation. Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), carries the warnings about concentrated economic power that popular summaries omit. The climate of your life was designed before you arrived.

Leadership as Seeing. Ronald Heifetz, Leadership Without Easy Answers (1994), distinguishes technical problems, solvable with existing knowledge, from adaptive challenges, which require changes in values, beliefs or behaviour; the practical version is Heifetz and Marty Linsky, Leadership on the Line (2002). Speaking uncomfortable truth inside an institution is among the most costly, and most necessary, forms of seeing.

Special Chapter: The Living Portrait

Helen Fisher, Why We Love (2004), reports the neuroimaging work mapping the dopamine systems involved in early romantic attraction. Ruth Feldman’s oxytocin research describes a different system, one of long-term bonding through trust, presence and proximity; it spans many papers rather than one landmark. These two systems want different things, and the tension between them runs through every long relationship.

Predictive processing, the reason the brain resists updating, is set out in Andy Clark, Surfing Uncertainty (2016), and in Karl Friston, “The Free-Energy Principle: A Unified Brain Theory?”, a paper in Nature Reviews Neuroscience (2010). The brain is a prediction machine, and confirmed predictions feel like truth. Kahneman’s work on cognitive ease shows that the familiar is perceived as true, which means your oldest snapshot of someone feels the most real even when it is the most outdated.

Gottman and Silver, The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work (1999), supplies the figures often quoted: couples who stayed together responded to bids for connection about 86% of the time, those who separated about 33%. Gottman’s predictive-accuracy claims have been criticised for fitting models to samples after the fact rather than testing them prospectively, so the figures are best read as a description of what the observed couples did, not as a forecast. Arthur Aron and Elaine Aron, Love and the Expansion of Self (1986), set out self-expansion theory, in which novel shared activities renew a relationship by creating new mutual seeing. Robert Levenson’s long-term couple satisfaction studies, conducted with Gottman over several decades, found that the willingness to tolerate discomfort during updating predicts who lasts.

The historical portraits in the chapter are Shah Jahan’s marble memorial to Mumtaz Mahal, begun in 1631; the correspondence of John and Abigail Adams; Octavian’s weaponised portrait of Cleopatra and Mark Antony; C.S. Lewis, A Grief Observed (1961); and Käthe Kollwitz’s eighteen years of work on The Grieving Parents, from 1914 to 1932. All are variations on the same question. What happens when the picture stops moving?

Afterword: The Calibrated Position

Configuration, context and process are the deeper architecture beneath everything in this volume, and they continue across the trilogy in The Full Room and The Gap. Marcus Aurelius and Etty Hillesum bookend the practice, one examining perception from the seat of absolute power, the other from the floor of absolute powerlessness. Both found the same observer.
