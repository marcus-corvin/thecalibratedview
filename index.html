You are arguing with someone you love. You don't know why it escalated. Something small, a tone or a word or a silence where a word should have been, and now you are both saying things that sound rehearsed. Like you've had this fight before. Like you're reading from a script neither of you wrote.

You are.

You did not choose how you love. You did not choose what success looks like to you. Both were installed before you could refuse them, and they have been running ever since.

Where this trilogy disagrees

Four positions it will not soften

Every one of these is argued at length inside the books, with the stories and the research to carry it. None of them is comfortable.

  1. “Who am I?” is a trap.

    Not because it is wrong to ask. Because it assumes a fixed answer is waiting to be uncovered, a true self buried under the noise that you could find if you searched hard enough. There isn't one. The better question points forward: what am I becoming?

    Volume I · The Ground Beneath

  2. Seeing clearly is not self-improvement.

    In the rooms where the healthcare system fails the sick and the justice system fails the wronged, unclear seeing is not an intellectual limitation. It is a condition with a body count.

    Volume I · Winter

  3. The world does not need more leaders.

    It needs more rooms that work without one. The measure is not how many people follow you. It is how many no longer need you, and whether the room still works after you leave it.

    Volume III · The Gap

  4. The better you lead, the faster you are forgotten.

    Larry Itliong built a coalition so strong it continued without him, and then without his name. That is the paradox at the heart of the work: the more effective the leader, the more the work can afford to forget them.

    Volume III · Fall

Konya, 1244

From Volume I — Summer

A scholar walks through the marketplace carrying an armload of books. He is Jalaluddin Rumi, a renowned jurist and theologian, a man whose entire identity rests on what he knows.

A wandering dervish steps into his path. Shams of Tabriz, ragged, owning nothing. He gestures at the books and asks a single question: what is the use of all this if it has not become living experience?

Rumi's books fall from his hands.

The Author

Marcus Corvin

Marcus Corvin is a pen name. The person behind it does not think that matters much.

He has spent years watching. Not the leaders on magazine covers, but the ones who organise the carpool, hold the meeting together when everyone wants to leave, and carry a weight nobody assigned. He has seen what it costs them, and what remains when they stop.

He has been the person in the room who notices the gap and stays seated. He has been the person who stands up. He has been both, sometimes in the same week.

He does not believe in heroes. He believes in rooms, and in the ordinary people who fill them.

Questions

Frequently asked

Is it really free?

Yes. All three volumes are free PDF downloads. No sign-up, no email list, no paywall. Licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.

What order should I read them in?

Volume I, then II, then III. Each stands alone, but the seasons build on each other, and Volume I's observer is the foundation for everything the later volumes ask of you.

Who is it for?

Anyone who wants to see more clearly, stay present through difficulty, or lead without waiting for permission. It has been written for students, parents, organisers, teachers and anyone who keeps showing up.

What is it about, exactly?

Perception, participation and leadership, told through real stories: Fannie Lou Hamer registering to vote in Mississippi, Jadav Payeng planting a forest on a sandbar, Septima Clark teaching one woman to write her name. The research is there to show the stories are not anecdotes.
